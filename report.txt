Academic burnout and cognitive overload are increasingly prevalent among students in higher 
education, driven by mounting academic pressures, information overload, and inadequate coping 
mechanisms. Early identification of these conditions is critical to enabling timely intervention 
and supporting student well -being. However, traditional approaches to detection rely on self -
reported surveys and retrospective assessments, which are often inac curate, delayed, and 
intrusive. There is a growing need for automated, non -intrusive systems capable of predicting 
burnout and cognitive overload in real time using observable behavioural signals.  
This project proposes a machine learning -based framework fo r the early prediction of academic 
burnout and cognitive overload by analysing student behavioural patterns. The system collects 
and processes multi -modal behavioural data including study session durations, interaction logs, 
assignment submission patterns,  browsing behaviour, and response times. Feature engineering 
techniques are applied to extract meaningful indicators such as irregularity in study schedules, 
declining engagement trends, and task -switching frequency, which are known correlates of 
burnout a nd overload.  
Classification models including Random Forest, Support Vector Machines, and LSTM -based 
deep learning networks are trained and evaluated on labelled student behavioural datasets. The 
system outputs early warning predictions at multiple risk lev els, enabling educators and 
counsellors to intervene proactively. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed 
approach achieves high prediction accuracy while maintaining low false -positive rates. The 
framework is designed to be lightweight, privacy -preserving, and deployable within existing 
Learning Management Systems, making it a practical tool for student wellness support in 
educational institutions.  v Table of Contents

Introduction  
Academic burnout and cognitive overload are among the most pressing challenges facing 
students in higher education today. The rap id expansion of academic curricula, increasing 
competitive pressures, digital information saturation, and the blurring of boundaries between 
study and personal life have collectively contributed to a growing mental health crisis among 
university students. Burnout, characterised by emotional exhaustion, depersonalisation, and a 
reduced sense of personal accomplishment, has been shown to significantly impair academic 
performance, mental well -being, and long -term career outcomes [1].  
Cognitive overload, a clos ely related phenomenon, occurs when the volume and complexity of 
information presented to a student exceeds the processing capacity of working memory. When 
students are consistently exposed to tasks that demand more cognitive resources than they 
possess, t heir ability to learn, retain, and apply knowledge deteriorates significantly [2]. Left 
undetected, both burnout and cognitive overload can escalate into serious psychological 
conditions including anxiety disorders and depression.  
The proliferation of digi tal learning environments, Learning Management Systems (LMS), and 
wearable devices has created an unprecedented opportunity to capture rich, continuous, and 
objective behavioural data from students. Patterns such as study session frequency and duration, 
assignment submission timings, login frequency, scrolling behaviour, response latencies, and 
interaction logs provide a real -time window into a student's cognitive and emotional state. When 
analysed using machine learning techniques, these behavioural signal s can serve as powerful 
early indicators of academic burnout and cognitive overload [4].  
This project proposes a comprehensive framework for the early prediction of academic burnout 
and cognitive overload through the automated analysis of student behaviour al data. The system 
collects multi -modal behavioural features, applies feature engineering and selection techniques, 
and trains multiple machine learning classifiers to generate early warning predictions. The CHAPTER 1. INTRODUCTION  
2 framework is designed to integrate seamlessly w ith existing LMS platforms and to operate in a 
privacy -preserving manner, making it practical for real -world deployment.  
It is important to recognise that burnout and cognitive overload, while conceptually distinct, are 
deeply interconnected in practice. C ognitive overload is fundamentally a short -term, task -level 
phenomenon rooted in the information -processing limits of working memory, whereas burnout 
is a longer -term, affective and motivational state that develops over weeks or months of 
sustained strain.  In practice, however, the two conditions frequently reinforce one another in a 
feedback loop: repeated episodes of cognitive overload leave a student feeling perpetually 
behind, which erodes motivation and self -efficacy, which in turn makes subsequent lea rning 
tasks feel even more cognitively demanding than they objectively are. A prediction system that 
focuses on behavioural signals is well positioned to capture this feedback loop, because many 
of the same observable indicators, such as declining engageme nt, erratic study timing, and rising 
task-switching frequency, are shared precursors of both conditions.  
The feasibility of behaviour -based early warning systems has also been shaped by a broader shift 
in how higher education is delivered. The accelerated adoption of hybrid and fully online 
learning models over the past several years has meant that a much larger share of a student's 
academic activity now leaves a digital trace than was the case a decade ago. Lecture attendance, 
resource access, formative qu izzes, discussion forum participation, and assignment submission 
increasingly occur through LMS platforms rather than in person. This shift is double -edged: it 
has removed many of the informal, face -to-face cues that instructors historically relied upon to  
notice a struggling student, such as visible fatigue or withdrawal in the classroom, while 
simultaneously making available a much richer stream of passive behavioural data than existed 
before. The framework proposed in this project is designed specificall y to take advantage of this 
shift, substituting the informal observational cues that are now harder to obtain with structured, 
continuously collected behavioural signals.  
1.1 Drawbacks of the Existing Solution  
Traditional approaches to identifying at -risk students rely heavily on self -reported surveys, 
periodic academic assessments, and one -on-one counselling sessions. These methods suffer 
from significant limitations:  
1. Reactive Rather than Proactive:  Existing methods act only after a problem has become 
visible. By the time a student is formally identified as experiencing burnout, the condition 
may already be severe and difficult to reverse [3].  CHAPTER 1. INTRODUCTION  
3 2. Reporting Bias and Lack of Granularity:  Self-reported data is prone to bias and recall 
errors, and these methods la ck the granularity needed for early and accurate detection.  
3. Infrequent, Blanket Survey Instruments:  Consider a student who experiences a 
sudden, sharp decline in engagement following an unexpected personal setback, such as 
a family emergency or a health is sue, midway through a semester. If the institution's only 
mechanism for detecting distress is a single end -of-semester wellness survey, this 
student's difficulties may go entirely unnoticed until final grades are already affected.  
4. Impractical at Scale:  Standardised psychometric instruments such as the Maslach 
Burnout Inventory remain the gold standard for clinical assessment, but their use in large -
scale monitoring is impractical due to the time and effort required for administration, the 
risk of survey fat igue, and the inability to provide real -time assessments [22].  
5. Costly and Intrusive Physiological Monitoring:  Physiological approaches are 
promising but costly and intrusive, and they require specialised hardware that is not yet 
widely available in educati onal settings [23].  
The points outline the key challenges and limitations of current approaches to detecting 
academic burnout and cognitive overload.

4 1.2 Motivation  
Several studies have reported that over 40% of university students experience clinically 
significan t levels of burnout during their academic careers [5]. The consequences extend beyond 
academic performance, affecting physical health, interpersonal relationships, and post -
graduation well -being. Despite this, most institutions lack automated, scalable sys tems capable 
of identifying students at risk before their condition worsens.  
The availability of digital behavioural data offers a unique opportunity to address this gap. 
Unlike self -reports, behavioural data is objective, continuous, and collected passive ly without 
interrupting the student. Machine learning models trained on such data can learn complex non -
linear patterns that are invisible to human observers, making them well -suited for early 
prediction tasks in educational settings.  
Beyond the direct imp act on individual students, institutions themselves carry a strong 
motivation to address burnout at scale. Elevated burnout within a cohort is associated with lower 
retention, higher withdrawal and repeat -enrolment rates, and reduced satisfaction scores in  
student surveys, all of which have downstream effects on an institution's academic standing, 
funding, and accreditation outcomes. From a purely operational perspective, even a modest 
improvement in early identification of at -risk students can translate in to meaningful reductions 
in attrition, making investment in automated monitoring systems financially as well as ethically 
justifiable for university administrations.  
A further motivation stems from the limitations of infrequent, blanket survey instruments.  A 
continuously operating behavioural monitoring system, in contrast to a single end -of-semester 
wellness survey, is capable of flagging a sudden change in activity pattern within days, creating 
a window for timely advisor outreach that a periodic survey s imply cannot provide.

The decision to focus specifically on undergraduate technology and science programme s was 
driven by two practical considerations. First, these programmes tend to have comparatively 
dense curricula, frequent formative assessment, and heavy reliance on digital submission 
platforms, which together produce a richer and more regular stream of behavioural data than is 
typically available in less digitally intensive disciplines. Second, prior literature reviewed in 
Chapter 2 indicates that burnout prevalence is particularly high in technically demanding fields, 
making this population both data -rich and practically significant for early intervention.

1.5 Definition of Key Terms  
To ensure clarity and consistency throughout this report, the following key terms are defined:  
• Academic Burnout:  A state of chronic physical and e motional exhaustion resulting from 
prolonged exposure to demanding academic workloads, characterised by reduced 
motivation, feelings of cynicism toward one's studies, and a perceived decline in 
academic competence.  
• Cognitive Overload:  A condition that aris es when the total cognitive load imposed by a 
learning task or environment exceeds the available capacity of the student's working 
memory, resulting in degraded task performance and reduced information retention.  
• Behavioural Analysis:  The systematic examin ation of observable student actions and 
patterns such as login frequency, submission timing, and resource access, to infer 
underlying cognitive or emotional states.  
• Learning Management System (LMS):  A software platform used by educational 
institutions to d eliver, manage, and track educational content, assignments, and student 
interactions. Examples include Moodle, Canvas, and Blackboard.  CHAPTER 1. INTRODUCTION  
7 • Feature Vector:  A numerical representation of a student's behavioural state for a given 
time window, constructed by aggre gating and transforming raw interaction events into a 
fixed -length array of features suitable for input to a machine learning model.  
• Risk Level:  A categorical label assigned by the prediction system to a student for a given 
time window, indicating the estimated likelihood of experiencing burnout or cognitive 
overload. Three levels are used: Low Risk, Moderate Risk, and High Risk.  
• Class Imbalance:  A characteristic of a labelled dataset in which the number of examples 
belonging to one class, such as High Risk, is substantially smaller than the number of 
examples belonging to other classes, which can bias a naively trained classifier toward 
the majori ty class.  
• Explainability (XAI):  The property of a machine learning system that allows its 
predictions to be interpreted and justified in terms understandable to a human user, 
typically through techniques such as SHAP values or attention weight visualisatio n, 
rather than being treated as an opaque black box.

2.1 Critical Evaluation of Available L iterature  
2.1.1  Academic Burnout: Theoretical Foundations  
The concept of burnout was first introduced by Freudenberger [6] and later formalised by 
Maslach and Jackson [7] through the development of the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI). 
The MBI defines burno ut along three dimensions: emotional exhaustion (feeling depleted of 
emotional resources), depersonalisation (developing a detached or cynical attitude toward one's 
work), and reduced personal accomplishment (a sense of ineffectiveness and failure). 
Subseq uent work adapted this framework to academic settings, resulting in the Maslach Burnout 
Inventory - Student Survey (MBI -SS) [8].  
Schaufeli et al. [9] further refined the academic burnout model by identifying exhaustion, 
cynicism, and academic inefficacy as  the core dimensions. Their longitudinal studies CHAPTER 2. LITERATURE SURVEY  
11 demonstrated that burnout develops gradually over time and is strongly correlated with study -
related stress, lack of autonomy, and poor social support. These findings underscore the 
importance of early detec tion before the condition becomes entrenched.  
Beyond the exhaustion -centred MBI framework, resource -based theories of occupational stress 
have also been influential in explaining how burnout develops. Conservation of Resources 
(COR) theory, originally prop osed by Hobfoll in the organisational psychology literature, 
models burnout as the consequence of a sustained net loss of personal resources, such as time, 
energy, and a sense of control, relative to the demands placed on an individual. Applied to the 
academic context, COR theory suggests that burnout is not simply the result of high workload 
in isolation, but of workload that persistently outpaces a student's ability to replenish the 
resources consumed by study, sleep, and social support. This resource -depletion framing is 
conceptually well aligned with the behavioural indicators used in this project, since declining 
engagement, increasing late -night study sessions, and reduced resource diversity can all be 
interpreted as symptoms of resources being deplete d faster than they are restored.  
A related and widely applied framework is the Job Demands -Resources (JD -R) model, which 
has been adapted to education as the Study Demands -Resources (SD -R) model. This model 
distinguishes between study demands, such as work load and time pressure, and study resources, 
such as autonomy, social support, and feedback quality, proposing that burnout arises when 
demands chronically exceed resources. The SD -R model is particularly useful for this project 
because it maps naturally o nto observable behavioural proxies: submission timing and session 
frequency can act as proxies for demand pressure, while engagement with optional or 
supplementary resources can act as a proxy for the degree of autonomy and resourcefulness a 
student is abl e to exercise under that pressure.  
2.1.2  Cognitive Overload in Learning Environments  
Cognitive Load Theory (CLT), introduced by Sweller [10], posits that human working memory 
has a limited capacity and that effective learning depends on managing the cognit ive load 
imposed by instructional materials. CLT distinguishes between intrinsic load (related to task 
complexity), extraneous load (caused by poor instructional design), and germane load (effort 
devoted to schema formation). Cognitive overload occurs when  the total load exceeds available 
working memory resources.  
In digital learning environments, cognitive overload is exacerbated by information 
fragmentation, multitasking demands, and notification interruptions. Researchers have identified CHAPTER 2. LITERATURE SURVEY  
12 behavioural indi cators of overload including increased task -switching frequency, longer 
response times, shorter study sessions with more frequent breaks, and declining engagement 
with learning materials [11]. These indicators form the basis for several features used in ou r 
prediction framework.  
Measuring cognitive load directly is methodologically challenging, and the literature broadly 
distinguishes between three families of measurement technique. Subjective rating instruments, 
such as the NASA Task Load Index (NASA -TLX),  ask learners to self -report perceived mental 
demand, effort, and frustration immediately after a task, and remain the most widely used 
approach due to their simplicity, though they share the recall -bias limitations of other self -report 
instruments discuss ed earlier in this chapter. Physiological measures, including pupillometry, 
electroencephalography (EEG), and galvanic skin response, offer objective, moment -to-moment 
estimates of cognitive strain but require specialised equipment that is rarely available  at scale in 
ordinary classroom or online learning settings. Behavioural proxy measures, by contrast, infer 
cognitive load indirectly from observable performance indicators such as response time 
variability, error rates, and task -switching frequency; these  measures are less precise at the 
individual -task level than physiological instruments, but they are the only family of 
measurement that can be captured passively and continuously from existing LMS infrastructure, 
which is why this project relies primarily  on behavioural proxies rather than subjective or 
physiological instruments.  
A further concept relevant to this project is the dual -task paradigm from cognitive psychology, 
which demonstrates that performance on a primary task degrades measurably when a se condary 
task competes for the same limited pool of working -memory resources. This principle has direct 
relevance to digital learning, where notifications, browser tabs, and messaging applications 
routinely compete with coursework for a student's attention.  The task -switching frequency and 
late-night session ratio features used later in this project's feature engineering pipeline (Chapter 
3) are designed specifically to capture behavioural symptoms of this kind of divided -attention 
overload.  
2.1.3  Burnout De tection Using Surveys and Physiological Signals  
The earliest approaches to burnout detection relied exclusively on standardised psychometric 
instruments. The Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI) and its derivatives remain the gold standard 
for clinical assessme nt. However, their use in large -scale monitoring is impractical due to the 
time and effort required for administration, the risk of survey fatigue, and the inability to provide 
real-time assessments [22].  CHAPTER 2. LITERATURE SURVEY  
13 Physiological approaches have been explored as obje ctive alternatives. Studies have 
demonstrated correlations between burnout and physiological markers such as elevated cortisol 
levels, heart rate variability (HRV), and skin conductance. Wearable devices capable of 
measuring these signals continuously have  enabled passive, real -time monitoring [23]. While 
promising, physiological approaches are costly, intrusive, and require specialised hardware that 
is not yet widely available in educational settings.  
2.1.4  Behavioural Indicators of Burnout and Cognitive O verload  
Research has identified a range of observable behavioural indicators associated with academic 
burnout and cognitive overload. These include:  
• Declining frequency and duration of study sessions, reflecting withdrawal and 
disengagement.  
• Increased procrastination, evidenced by late submission of assignments and last -minute 
login activity spikes before deadlines.  
• Reduced variety in learning activities, such as accessing fewer unique resources or 
modules.  
• Increased response latency in interactive exer cises, indicating slower cognitive 
processing.  
• Higher task -switching frequency within study sessions, suggesting difficulty in 
maintaining focus.  
• Declining scores on formative assessments, reflecting reduced knowledge retention.  
Kim et al. [24] validated these indicators against self -reported burnout scores using a dataset of 
312 university students, finding statistically significant correlations for all six indicators listed 
above. Their findings directly informed the feature engineering approach used in this project.  
2.1.5  Learning Management System Data Analysis  
Modern LMS platforms such as Moodle, Canvas, and Blackboard generate rich logs of student 
activity. Each log entry records the student identifier, timestamp, event type (e.g., resource view, 
quiz submission, forum post), and contextual metadata. Aggregated over time, these logs provide 
a detailed picture of a student's engagement trajectory.  
Romero and Ventura [25] conducted a comprehensive survey of data mining applications in 
LMS environments, r eviewing over 100 studies and identifying common patterns in at -risk 
student behaviour. They found that engagement metrics derived from LMS logs were among the CHAPTER 2. LITERATURE SURVEY  
14 most reliable predictors of academic outcomes. More recent work has extended this analysis to 
mental health prediction, demonstrating that the same engagement metrics are also predictive of 
burnout and stress states [26].  
2.1.6  Machine Learning in Educational Data Mining  
Educational Data Mining (EDM) is an interdisciplinary field that applies data mi ning and 
machine learning techniques to data generated in educational environments. Early work in EDM 
focused on predicting student performance and dropout using demographic and academic 
records [12]. More recent work has shifted toward predicting student well-being and mental 
health states using LMS interaction data.  
Across the EDM literature, a relatively small number of algorithm families recur repeatedly: 
tree-based ensemble methods such as Random Forest and Gradient Boosting, kernel methods 
such as Sup port Vector Machines, and, increasingly, neural network architectures suited to 
sequential or high -dimensional data. Tree -based ensembles remain popular in this domain 
because they tend to perform well on the moderately sized, tabular, and often noisy data sets 
typical of institutional LMS exports, and because they offer built -in feature importance measures 
that support the explainability requirements discussed in Section 1.5. Evaluation practice in this 
literature has also converged on a standard set of con cerns beyond raw accuracy, including 
stratified cross -validation to guard against overly optimistic estimates from a single train -test 
split, and explicit class -imbalance handling, since at -risk students are almost always a minority 
class in any realistic student population, a point that directly motivates the class -balancing 
strategy described in Chapter 3.  
Hlosta et al. [13] applied Random Forest classifiers to Moodle LMS data to predict at -risk 
students with an accuracy of 76%. Jayaprakash et al. [14] us ed logistic regression and decision 
trees on open university data and found that early submission behaviour was among the strongest 
predictors of academic outcomes. Tempelaar et al. [15] investigated the relationship between 
learning dispositions and LMS b ehaviour, finding significant correlations between activity 
patterns and academic achievement.  
More directly relevant to our work, Cao et al. [16] proposed a multi -modal framework for student 
stress detection using smartphone sensing data including acceler ometer, GPS, and app usage 
patterns. Their Support Vector Machine model achieved an F1 -score of 0.81 on a dataset of 48 
university students. Similarly, Wang et al. [17] used passive smartphone data to predict CHAPTER 2. LITERATURE SURVEY  
15 depression among college students, demonstratin g that behavioural patterns captured through 
mobile devices are strong proxies for mental health states.  
2.1.7  Deep Learning for Sequential Behavioural Data  
The temporal nature of behavioural data makes it well -suited for sequence modelling 
approaches. Rec urrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and their variants, particularly Long Short -
Term Memory (LSTM) networks [18], have demonstrated strong performance on sequential data 
across domains including activity recognition, healthcare monitoring, and student modelling.  
Piech et al. [19] introduced Deep Knowledge Tracing, an LSTM -based model for tracking 
student knowledge over time, demonstrating that deep learning models can capture complex 
temporal dependencies in student interaction data. Subsequent work by Zhang et a l. [20] applied 
bidirectional LSTMs to predict student dropout from MOOC platforms, achieving an AUC -ROC 
of 0.89. These results motivate our use of LSTM as one of the core prediction models in this 
project.  
More recently, attention -based architectures and Transformer models have emerged as a natural 
extension of recurrent sequence models, offering the ability to weight the contribution of each 
time step directly rather than propagating information through a recurrent hidden state. In 
principle, a Transforme r-based encoder could replace the LSTM used in this project and might 
offer modest accuracy improvements on sufficiently large datasets. In practice, Transformer 
architectures typically require substantially larger volumes of labelled training data to 
outperform recurrent alternatives, and their self -attention weights, while interpretable in 
principle, are less directly aligned with the per -day attention visualisation described in Section 
3.13 than the simpler attention mechanism added to the LSTM's final l ayer. Given the moderate 
dataset size available for this project, described in Section 3.3, an LSTM architecture was judged 
to offer the better trade -off between predictive performance, training stability, and 
interpretability, with a Transformer -based arc hitecture identified as a natural direction for future 
work once larger multi -institutional datasets become available.  
2.1.8  Natural Language Processing Approaches  
Beyond structured interaction logs, unstructured text data generated by students in online 
learning environments has been investigated as a source of early warning signals. Forum posts, 
assignment submission comments, and chat messages contain linguistic markers of cognitive 
and emotional states that complement behavioural indicators derived from  click -stream data.  CHAPTER 2. LITERATURE SURVEY  
16 Sentiment analysis applied to student forum posts has been shown to predict academic 
disengagement with moderate accuracy [32]. Students experiencing burnout tend to post less 
frequently, use more negative sentiment language, and exhibi t decreased linguistic complexity 
over time. Topic modelling approaches such as Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) have been 
used to identify thematic shifts in student writing that correlate with increased stress levels. For 
instance, a shift from content -focused posts discussing lecture material to process -focused posts 
asking for extensions or expressing overwhelm is a consistent early indicator of distress.  
Transformer -based language models such as BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations 
from Transformers) have recently been applied to educational text data with promising results. 
Malhotra et al. [33] fine -tuned a BERT model on student forum posts from three MOOC 
platforms and achieved an F1 -score of 0.84 for predicting disengagement within the  subsequent 
two weeks. While NLP -based approaches are not the primary focus of our system, they represent 
a natural extension of the multi -modal framework proposed here and are identified as a direction 
for future work.  
2.1.9  Transfer Learning and Domain A daptation  
A practical challenge in deploying machine learning models for student burnout prediction is the 
availability of labelled training data. Labelling student behavioural records with burnout severity 
scores requires either clinical assessment or val idated psychometric instruments, both of which 
are resource -intensive. This creates a significant barrier to deployment, particularly for smaller 
institutions that lack the research infrastructure to generate large labelled datasets.  
Transfer learning appr oaches address this challenge by pre -training models on data from one 
institution or domain and fine -tuning them on limited labelled data from the target institution. 
Park et al. [34] demonstrated that an LSTM model pre -trained on LMS interaction data from  one 
university could be fine -tuned with as few as 50 labelled examples from a target university while 
retaining 89% of the performance achieved with full supervision. This finding has important 
implications for the scalability of our system, suggesting th at a single well -trained base model 
could be adapted to new institutional contexts with minimal additional labelling effort.  
Domain adaptation techniques such as Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) minimisation and 
adversarial training have also been applied to  address distributional shift between source and 
target institutions, where differences in LMS platform, academic culture, or student population 
may cause the feature distributions to differ significantly [35]. These techniques represent CHAPTER 2. LITERATURE SURVEY  
17 promising directio ns for enhancing the generalisability of the proposed system beyond its 
training institution.  
2.1.10  Comparison with Existing Systems  
Several commercial and research systems have been proposed for student monitoring. EAP 
(Early Alert Predictive) systems de ployed by institutions such as Purdue University and Georgia 
State University focus primarily on academic performance and dropout prediction. Student 
Success Systems like EAB Navigate and Civitas Learning use predictive analytics to identify at -
risk studen ts, but their models are proprietary and do not specifically target burnout or cognitive 
overload [27].  
In the research literature, the COMPASS system by Makransky et al. [28] used physiological 
and behavioural signals to detect cognitive overload in virtu al reality learning environments, 
achieving a classification accuracy of 82%. The Student Wellbeing Monitor by Ren et al. [29] 
combined LMS data with smartphone sensing to predict student mental health states with an F1 -
score of 0.79. Our system builds on these foundations while extending the feature set, improving 
the prediction pipeline, and targeting a broader range of risk levels.  
2.1.11  Privacy and Ethics in Student Monitoring Systems  
The deployment of automated student monitoring systems raises import ant ethical questions 
regarding privacy, consent, and the potential for algorithmic bias. Prinsloo and Slade [30] 
identify three core ethical tensions in learning analytics: the tension between institutional benefit 
and individual privacy, the tension betw een data -driven decision -making and human agency, 
and the tension between standardised models and individual diversity.  
Regarding privacy, most jurisdictions require that student data be collected and processed in 
accordance with data protection legislatio n. In the United States, the Family Educational Rights 
and Privacy Act (FERPA) governs the use of educational records. In Europe, the General Data 
Protection Regulation (GDPR) applies. Both frameworks require that data minimisation 
principles be observed, meaning that only the minimum data necessary for the stated purpose 
should be collected. Our system is designed to comply with these principles by operating 
exclusively on anonymised interaction logs and explicitly excluding personally identifiable data 
from all model inputs.  
Regarding algorithmic bias, machine learning models trained on historical data can inadvertently 
encode and amplify existing demographic disparities. For example, if students from certain 
ethnic or socioeconomic groups have historicall y lower LMS engagement due to factors CHAPTER 2. LITERATURE SURVEY  
18 unrelated to burnout such as unreliable internet access, a model trained on this data may 
incorrectly assign higher risk scores to these groups. Addressing this concern requires careful 
bias auditing during model evalu ation, which should include disaggregated performance analysis 
across demographic subgroups [31].

This chapter has reviewed the theoretical foundation s of academic burnout and cognitive

behavioural data, and synthesised the quantitative results reported across this body of work. The

outcomes. While considerable progress has been m ade in developing theoretical models of 
burnout and in applying machine learning to educational data, several important gaps remain.  
First, the majority of prior prediction systems focus on academic performance outcomes such as

with most studies omitting discussion of privacy, consent, and bias. Additionally, many existing 
models are evaluated on small or non -representati ve datasets, limiting their generalisability [21].

combining multi -modal data sources, employing LSTM -based sequence modelling, and 
incorporating a privacy -preserving architecture with explicit consideration of ethical concerns.

3.1 Problem Formulation  
Academic burnout and cognitive overload are prevalent among university students yet remain 
largely undetected until they have significan tly impaired student well -being and academic 
performance. Existing detection methods are reactive, infrequent, and rely on self -reported data 
that is prone to bias and recall errors. There is a critical need for an automated, objective, and 
proactive syste m that can identify students at risk of burnout and cognitive overload at an early 
stage.  
Formally, the problem can be stated as follows. Given a time -series of behavioural observations 
B = {b1, b2, ..., bt} for a student over a period of t days, where eac h observation bi is a feature 
vector derived from the student's LMS interactions, submission behaviour, and micro -survey 
responses, the goal is to learn a function f: B → Y, where Y ∈ {Low Risk, Moderate Risk, High 
Risk}, that predicts the student's burnou t and cognitive overload risk level with high accuracy 
and low false -negative rate.  
The key challenges addressed by this project are:  
• Feature Engineering:  Identifying and constructing behavioural features from raw 
interaction logs that are semantically mea ningful and predictive of burnout and cognitive 
overload states.  CHAPTER 3. PROJECT FLOW AND METHODOLOGY  
21 • Class Imbalance:  High -risk students represent a minority class in any student 
population. The prediction model must handle this imbalance without sacrificing recall 
on the minority class.  
• Tem poral Dependencies:  Burnout and overload develop over time. The model must be 
capable of capturing temporal patterns and trends rather than relying solely on 
instantaneous snapshots.  
• Generalisation:  The model must generalise across students with different academic 
backgrounds, study habits, and personal circumstances.  
• Privacy:  The system must operate without collecting sensitive personal data and must 
comply with educational data privacy regulations.  
The proposed solution addresses these challenges through a combination of robust feature 
engineering, class -balanced training strategies, LSTM -based sequence modelling, and a privacy -
preserving data pipeline that operates entirely on anonymised behavioura l logs.  
3.2 System Overview  
The proposed system operates as a background service integrated with the institution's LMS. 
Student interaction data is collected continuously and processed in near -real-time. The system 
generates a daily risk score for each student, classified into one of three risk levels: Low Risk 
(Green), Moderate Risk (Amber), and High Risk (Red). When a student's risk level reaches 
Moderate or High, an alert is generated and sent to the student's academic advisor or support 
counsellor.  
The system is designed with privacy at its core. All data is anonymised at collection, stored in 
encrypted form, and access is restricted to authorised personnel. No personally identifiable 
information is used in model training or inference. The system comp lies with FERPA (Family 
Educational Rights and Privacy Act) and GDPR guidelines for educational data.

• Session segmentation:  Consecutive events with inter -event gaps of less than 30 minutes

3.9 Evaluation Metrics  
Models are evaluated using the following metrics computed on the held -out test set: Accuracy 
(overall proportion of correct predictions), Precision (weighted average across classes), Recall 
(weighted average across classes, with emphasis on the High Risk c lass), F1 -Score (harmonic 
mean of precision and recall), and AUC -ROC (area under the receiver operating characteristic 
curve, computed using the one -vs-rest strategy for multi -class classification). For safety -critical 
applications like mental health predi ction, recall on the High Risk class is prioritised to minimise 
false negatives.  
3.10 Validation Strategy and Experimental Protocol  
To ensure that reported results are reliable and reproducible, a rigorous experimental protocol is 
followed throughout the e valuation. All experiments use a fixed random seed (seed = 42) for 
any stochastic operations including train -test splitting, SMOTE oversampling, Random Forest CHAPTER 3. PROJECT FLOW AND METHODOLOGY  
28 construction, and LSTM weight initialisation. This ensures that results are exactly reproducible 
across runs.  
The dataset is split into training, validation, and test sets once before any model training begins, 
and the test set is used exclusively for final evaluation. No hyperparameter tuning or model 
selection decisions are made based on test set pe rformance, preventing information leakage from 
the test set into the model development process. Validation set performance is used to guide 
hyperparameter tuning and early stopping.  
For the cross -validation experiments reported in Section 4.6, stratified k -fold splitting is applied 
with k=5 to ensure that each fold preserves the class distribution of the original dataset. 
Performance metrics are computed separately for each fold and the mean and standard deviation 
across folds are reported. The relatively s mall standard deviations observed confirm that the 
reported performance figures are stable estimates rather than artifacts of a particular data split.  
Statistical significance testing using McNemar's test is performed to confirm that the observed 
performan ce differences between models are statistically significant at the p = 0.05 level. The 
LSTM model is found to be significantly better than both RF and SVM on the primary metric of 
High Risk recall, confirming that the performance advantage of LSTM is not d ue to random 
variation.

3.12 System Integration Architecture  
The proposed system is designed to integrate with any LMS that exposes a standard REST API 
for log r etrieval. The integration layer is implemented as a lightweight Python microservice that 
polls the LMS API on a daily schedule using a cron job. The service authenticates using OAuth 
2.0 tokens managed by the institution's identity provider, ensuring that data access is auditable 
and revocable.  
All communication between system components uses HTTPS with TLS 1.3 encryption. Student 
data is stored in a PostgreSQL database with row -level security policies ensuring that no user 
can access records outside their authorised scope. The prediction model is deployed as a REST 
API endpoint using FastAPI, which receives feature vectors and returns risk level predictions 
with associated confidence scores. The entire system is containerised using Docker and can be 
deploye d on any cloud or on -premises infrastructure with minimal configuration.  
3.13 Model Interpretability and Explainability  
In safety -critical applications such as student mental health prediction, model interpretability is 
not merely a desirable property but an ethical requirement. Advisors and counsellors must be 
able to understand why a particular student has been flagged as high risk before they take action. 
Black -box predictions without supporting explanations risk eroding trust, causing inappropriate 
interventions, or missing the root cause of a student's difficulties.  
To address this requirement, the proposed system integrates SHAP (SHapley Additive 
exPlanations) for the Random Forest model and attention weight visualisation for the LSTM CHAPTER 3. PROJECT FLOW AND METHODOLOGY  
31 model. SHAP values decompose each prediction into a sum of feature contributions, providing 
a ranked list of the behavioural indicators that most strongly influenced the risk classification 
for each individual student. For example, a high -risk prediction driven primar ily by a sharply 
declining engagement trend and a high percentage of late submissions would suggest workload 
management difficulties, whereas one driven primarily by micro -survey scores indicating low 
energy and poor sleep would suggest a physiological str ess response warranting different support 
strategies.  
For the LSTM model, the attention mechanism added to the second LSTM layer produces a 
weight for each day in the 14 -day observation window, indicating which days contributed most 
to the final prediction . Visualising these attention weights as a heatmap over the observation 
window allows advisors to identify specific events or periods, such as the week following a 
major exam or the days surrounding a personal event, that may have triggered the onset of 
burnout.

4.7 Discussion  
The experimental results demonstrate that the proposed framework is capable of predicting 
academic burnout and cognitive overload risk with high accuracy and strong recall on the High 
Risk class. The LSTM model's superior performance validates the hypothes is that temporal 
dependencies in student behavioural data carry significant predictive information that cannot be 
captured by static feature -based classifiers.  
The ablation study reveals that the combination of LMS -derived behavioural features and micro -
survey features is more powerful than either source alone. This finding supports a multi -modal 
approach to burnout prediction and suggests that even lightweight survey instruments (five 
questions bi -weekly) contribute meaningfully to prediction accuracy.  
The two-week observation window finding has practical implications for deployment. A system 
that requires only two weeks of data before generating its first prediction can identify at -risk 
students early in the semester, leaving ample time for intervention. T his is a significant 
advantage over semester -end surveys that provide no opportunity for early action.

5.1 Conclusion  
This project presented an automated framework for the earl y prediction of academic burnout and 
cognitive overload in university students using behavioural analysis and machine learning. The 
system collects multi -modal behavioural data from LMS interaction logs, assignment 
submission records, and bi -weekly micro -surveys, extracts 24 engineered features across four 
categories, and trains three machine learning models (Random Forest, SVM, and LSTM) to 
predict student risk levels.  
The experimental results on a dataset of 423 students over one academic semester demonst rate 
that the LSTM -based model achieves an overall accuracy of 87.3%, a High Risk class recall of 
91.2%, and an AUC -ROC of 0.93 for the High Risk class. These results represent a significant 
improvement over prior work and establish the feasibility of auto mated, non -intrusive burnout 
prediction using passively collected behavioural data.  
The key contributions of this project are:  
• A comprehensive multi -modal feature engineering framework that combines LMS 
engagement metrics, temporal behavioural patterns, su bmission behaviour indicators, 
and micro -survey responses into a unified 24 -feature representation.  
• An LSTM -based sequence model that effectively captures temporal dependencies in 
student behavioural data, outperforming traditional static classifiers on al l evaluation 
metrics.  
• An empirical analysis demonstrating that a 14 -day observation window is sufficient for 
reliable burnout prediction, enabling early -semester risk identification.  
• A modular, privacy -preserving system architecture designed for practical deployment 
within existing LMS infrastructure.  CHAPTER 5. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORKS  
43 5.2 Limitations  
The current system has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the dataset was 
collected from a single institution and may not generalise to students at different types of 
universities or in different cultural contexts. Second, the ground truth labels are derived from 
end-of-period surveys and counsellor assessments, which may not perfectly capture burnout 
states at earlier time points. Third, the system currently generates predictions at the day -week 
level and does not support real -time in -session monitoring.  
5.3 Future Prospects  
Several directions for future work are identified. First, the system should be validated on multi -
institutional datasets to assess its generalisabi lity. Second, the integration of additional data 
sources such as physiological signals from wearables and natural language features from student 
forum posts could further improve prediction accuracy. Third, the development of personalised 
prediction models  that adapt to individual student baselines and trajectories is a promising 
research direction. Fourth, the design of evidence -based intervention strategies triggered by the 
system's predictions, in collaboration with academic counsellors, is a critical ne xt step toward 
real-world impact. Finally, longitudinal evaluation of the system's effectiveness in reducing 
burnout prevalence in deployed settings remains an important open challenge.  
5.4 Recommendations for Institutional Deployment  
Based on the findings  of this project, the following recommendations are made for institutions 
considering deploying the proposed system or similar automated burnout prediction 
frameworks:  
• Pilot before scaling:  Begin deployment in a single course or department with a small 
cohort of students and a dedicated academic advisor partner. Use the pilot to validate 
system performance in the local context, identify any data quality issues in the 
institution's LMS logs, and gather feedback from both students and advisors before 
scaling to the full institution.  
• Establish clear data governance policies:  Before deployment, institutions should 
establish and communicate clear policies governing what data is collected, how it is 
stored, who has access, how long it is retained, and under what c ircumstances it is 
deleted. These policies should be developed in consultation with data protection officers, 
student representatives, and academic staff.  CHAPTER 5. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORKS  
44 • Prioritise explainability in advisor interfaces:  Advisors should not be expected to act 
on unexplained risk scores. The dashboard interface should always display the top 
contributing factors to each student's risk classification, allowing advisors to corroborate 
the system's assessment with their own ob servations and to tailor their intervention 
accordingly.  
• Invest in counsellor capacity alongside the system:  An automated prediction system 
can only deliver benefit if the institution has sufficient counselling and support capacity 
to respond to the alerts  it generates. Deploying a prediction system without expanding 
support capacity risks creating a situation where students are identified as at -risk but are 
placed on long waiting lists for counselling, which may worsen their distress.  
• Conduct regular model  retraining and bias auditing:  The behavioural patterns 
associated with burnout may shift over time as academic culture, LMS platforms, and 
student demographics evolve. Models should be retrained periodically on fresh data, and 
each new model version shoul d be subjected to a bias audit examining performance 
across demographic subgroups before deployment.  
• Obtain informed consent from students:  While LMS interaction data is routinely 
collected by institutions for administrative purposes, using it for mental h ealth prediction 
represents a new use case that students may not anticipate. Institutions should obtain 
explicit informed consent from students before enrolling them in the prediction system, 
and should provide clear opt -out mechanisms.

The framework developed in this project has implications that extend beyond academic burnout

student well -being monitoring tasks including detection of social isolation, academic dishonesty 
risk, and mental health deterioration more generally.

to leverage technology not merely to increase productivity or efficiency but to actively promote

based manner.  CHAPTER 5. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORKS
